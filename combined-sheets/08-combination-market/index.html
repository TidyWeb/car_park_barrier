Exercise 01

Five traders have booked pitches at the Saturday market. Each stall is a dictionary and all five sit inside a list. Frontage is the width of the pitch, in feet.

Given

stalls = [
    {"trader": "Robin Banks", "feet": 6, "power": False},
    {"trader": "Paige Turner", "feet": 10, "power": True},
    {"trader": "Wayne Kerr", "feet": 4, "power": True},
    {"trader": "Barry Tone", "feet": 12, "power": False},
    {"trader": "Joe King", "feet": 8, "power": True},
]

Print the trader on the third stall, then the frontage of that same stall, then the frontage of the last one. Three lines of output.

Expected output

Wayne Kerr
4
8
Stuck?

The first bracket picks a stall out of the list by its number. The second asks that stall for one of its fields by name. Counting starts at nought.

Exercise 02

Pitches with electricity have to be laid out near the generator, so the market clerk needs to know which ones those are.

Reach in for Barry Tone's stall and print one sentence or the other, depending on what you find.

Expected output

Barry Tone does not need electricity.
Stuck?

The value stored under "power" is already True or False. An if can test it as it stands, and the else catches everything it does not.

Exercise 03

A pitch costs £4 for every foot of frontage. Electricity adds a flat £12 on top.

Work out what Paige Turner pays and print the number on its own. No sentence yet.

Expected output

52

Nothing in the data holds the fee. It does not exist until you work it out.

Stuck?

Work out the frontage first and put it in a variable of its own. The electricity is a separate decision made afterwards, and it only ever adds.

Exercise 04

Every stall needs its fee working out, and the clerk will be asked about them one at a time all morning. Printing them all now would not help — by the time someone asks about Wayne Kerr, the line has scrolled away.

Use a new "fee" field for each stall. As the loop visits a stall, calculate its fee and store it on that stall. The loop itself prints nothing.

Then, after the loop has finished, read the stored fee from Wayne Kerr's stall and print a sentence with the money to two decimal places.

A dictionary can grow when you assign a value to a new key. Reading a missing key is different: it raises an error. Here, assignment is what creates the new "fee" field.

Expected output

Wayne Kerr pays £28.00.
Stuck?

The new field needs a name: use "fee". Think about which side of an assignment stores a value rather than reads one, and keep that assignment inside the loop.

Still stuck?

After the loop, Wayne Kerr's dictionary should contain one extra field. Retrieve that field after every stall has been visited.
